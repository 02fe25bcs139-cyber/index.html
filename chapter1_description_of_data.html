Section 1.1

What is Data &
Types of Variables

Data refers to a collection of facts, figures, or information gathered through observation, measurement, or experiment. It is the raw material of statistics — numbers, words, or measurements that must be organized and interpreted to yield knowledge.

In statistics, data is broadly classified into Qualitative (Categorical) and Quantitative (Numerical) types. Qualitative data describes categories or qualities (e.g., blood type, gender), while Quantitative data represents measurable quantities (e.g., height, temperature).

Variables are characteristics that can take different values across observations. A variable can be Discrete (countable values — like number of children: 0, 1, 2…) or Continuous (any value in a range — like weight: 65.4 kg, 65.41 kg…).

Further classification includes Nominal (labels without order: colors, names), Ordinal (ordered categories: rankings), Interval (equal gaps, no true zero: temperature in °C), and Ratio (equal gaps + true zero: height, weight) scales.

Measures of Central
Tendency & Dispersion

Mean

The Arithmetic Mean is the sum of all observations divided by the number of observations. It uses every data value and is sensitive to outliers.

The Weighted Mean assigns different weights to values, useful when some data points matter more (e.g., CGPA calculation with credit hours as weights).

x̄ = Σxᵢ / n x̄w = Σwᵢxᵢ / Σwᵢ
📍

Median & Mode

The Median is the middle value when data is sorted. It is robust to outliers — ideal for skewed data like income or house prices.

The Mode is the most frequently occurring value. A dataset can be unimodal, bimodal, or multimodal. Mode is the only measure applicable to nominal data.

M = (n+1)/2 th value Mode = most frequent
🔢

Quartiles &
Percentiles

Quartiles divide sorted data into 4 equal parts. Q1 (25th percentile), Q2 (Median, 50th), Q3 (75th). The IQR = Q3 – Q1 measures the spread of the middle 50%.

Percentiles divide data into 100 equal parts. The 90th percentile means 90% of data falls below that value.

IQR = Q3 − Q1
📐

Variance &
Std. Deviation

Variance (σ²) measures the average squared deviation from the mean. A higher variance means data is more spread out.

Standard Deviation (σ) is the square root of variance, expressed in the same units as the data. It is the most commonly used measure of spread.

σ² = Σ(xᵢ−x̄)² / n σ = √σ²
📏

Range & IQR

The Range is the simplest measure of spread: the difference between the largest and smallest values. It is easy to compute but highly sensitive to outliers.

Inter-Quartile Range (IQR) measures the spread of the central 50% of the data and is unaffected by extreme values. It is used to detect outliers via the fence method.

Range = Max − Min Fence = Q3 + 1.5×IQR
📉

Coefficient of
Variation

CV expresses the standard deviation as a percentage of the mean. It is a dimensionless number used to compare variability between datasets with different units or scales.

Example: Comparing variability in exam scores (out of 100) vs test times (in seconds) — CV makes them comparable.

CV = (σ / x̄) × 100%
Section 1.5

Box Plot (Box & Whisker)

A Box Plot is a graphical summary using five key statistics: Minimum, Q1, Median (Q2), Q3, and Maximum. The box spans Q1 to Q3 (the IQR), the line inside the box is the median, and the whiskers extend to the smallest/largest non-outlier values. Points beyond the fences are plotted as individual outlier dots.

The box plot above shows: minimum=10, Q1=20, Median=35, Q3=50, Maximum=65. The outlier at ~67 exceeds the upper fence (Q3 + 1.5×IQR = 50 + 1.5×30 = 95). IQR = Q3 − Q1 = 30. The median is closer to Q1, suggesting a slight positive (right) skew.

Section 1.6

Histogram

A Histogram is a bar graph showing the frequency distribution of continuous data. The horizontal axis represents class intervals (bins) and the vertical axis shows frequency (count) or relative frequency. Unlike bar charts, the bars are adjacent (touching) since the data is continuous. The shape of a histogram reveals the distribution's symmetry, skewness, and modality.

← Class Intervals (e.g., marks: 0–10, 10–20 …) · Each bar = frequency of that interval →

Section 1.7

Skewness

Skewness measures the asymmetry of a distribution. It tells us whether the tail of the distribution is stretched to the left or right of the peak. Karl Pearson's coefficient: Sk = 3(Mean − Median) / σ. A symmetric distribution has Sk = 0.

Positive (Right) Skew

Tail extends to the right. Mean > Median > Mode. Common in income distributions, house prices. Most data is clustered at lower values with few very high outliers pulling the mean rightward.

Sk > 0

Symmetric (Normal)

The distribution is perfectly balanced. Mean = Median = Mode. The bell-shaped Normal distribution is the most important symmetric distribution in statistics and forms the basis for many hypothesis tests.

Sk = 0

Negative (Left) Skew

Tail extends to the left. Mean < Median < Mode. Occurs when most values are high and a few extremely low outliers drag the mean leftward. Common in exam scores when most students score high.

Sk < 0
Section 1.8

Normal Q-Q Plot

A Normal Quantile-Quantile (Q-Q) Plot is a graphical tool to assess whether a dataset follows a Normal distribution. It plots the quantiles of the observed data (y-axis) against the theoretical quantiles of a Normal distribution (x-axis).

How to read it: If the data is approximately Normal, the points will fall close to the reference diagonal line. Deviations from this line reveal departures from Normality — such as heavy tails, light tails, or skewness.

S-shaped curves indicate skewness; curves bending above or below the line suggest heavy or light tails (kurtosis). The Q-Q plot is widely used before applying tests that assume Normality (t-test, ANOVA).

Most points on the reference line → approximately Normal

Section 1.9

Coefficient of Variation &
Relative Measures

Coefficient of Variation (CV)

CV = (Standard Deviation / Mean) × 100%. It is a relative measure of dispersion — dimensionless, so it allows comparison of variability across datasets with different units or magnitudes. A lower CV indicates more consistency; a higher CV indicates more variability.

Dataset A (Scores)CV = 12%
Dataset B (Times)CV = 34%
Dataset C (Prices)CV = 58%

Dataset A is most consistent (low CV). Dataset C is most variable.
